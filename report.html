Zambia · Livestock Economy Research & Deployment Brief Sept 2026

What does a $10 billion beef export industry mean for Zambia?

The country is chasing its first $1 billion. This is a map of the order of magnitude beyond it — the herd, the rails, the cold chain, the power, and the money.

Scope  Herd · Rail · Logistics · Energy · Finance Anchor herd  5.1M → ~46M cattle Ocean gates  TAZARA · Lobito
01

From a slogan to a scale

Zambia has set itself a real target: at least US$1 billion in beef exports a year by around 2031. The first formal consignment — 55 head from Namwala, slaughtered at Kalomo — crossed into the DRC in October 2025.1,2

That is the on-ramp. The question here is the destination one order of magnitude further out: a $10 billion beef export economy. Not as a forecast, but as a way to see the whole system at once — because at $10B the constraints stop being about marketing and start being about biology, steel, and electricity.

The headline finding is simple and sobering. A $1B industry is a commercial upgrade of what Zambia already has. A $10B industry is a national transformation on the scale of two to three decades: a herd approaching Argentina's, export earnings approaching Australia's, and a cold-chain-grade logistics network laid over rails that were built to move copper, not chilled meat.

02

The arithmetic of $10 billion

Work backwards from the dollar figure and the physical demands become unavoidable. Ten billion dollars of beef is roughly 1.7 million tonnes of product a year — and behind every tonne stand live animals, off-take, and a herd big enough to sustain the slaughter.

At an internationally traded price of about US$6,000 a tonne for deboned, chilled/frozen export beef,5,6 $10B is ~1.67 million tonnes. If each slaughtered animal yields ~180 kg of saleable export beef — itself requiring carcass weights roughly double today's smallholder average — that is ~9.3 million head slaughtered for export every year. Add domestic and regional non-premium demand and total commercial slaughter runs near 13 million head a year.

A herd can only be slaughtered as fast as it reproduces. At a world-class off-take of ~28%, sustaining 13M head of annual slaughter requires a national herd of roughly 46 million cattle — about nine times today's. Move the sliders below to see how sensitive that is.

03

Where the cattle already are

Zambia's herd is not scattered evenly. It is concentrated in the Southern Province — and, crucially, along the very corridor the main railway already follows.

A handful of Southern Province districts hold close to half the national herd. Monze, Itezhi-Tezhi, Namwala and Kalomo alone run well over a million head between them.7 Western Province (Barotseland) carries a large traditional herd too, but it sits off the rail grid and inside Zambia's oldest foot-and-mouth zone — a market-access problem before it is a logistics one.8

04

Rails built for copper — bent toward beef

Zambia's railways were engineered to move minerals out and fuel in. The good news: the alignment already serves cattle country. The gap is not track — it is temperature.

The Zambia Railways spine runs ~846 km on 1,067 mm gauge from Livingstone through Choma, Monze, Mazabuka, Lusaka and Kabwe up to the Copperbelt.9 It threads the cattle belt end to end. From Kapiri Mposhi, two ocean gates open:

TAZARA — the eastern gate

Kapiri Mposhi to Dar es Salaam, ~1,860 km. A US$1.4B revitalisation (CCECC, 30-year concession) signed Sept 2025 aims to lift freight capacity from 0.2 to 2.4 million tonnes/year within three years and cut transit times by two-thirds, with 34 new locomotives and 760 wagons.10,11 Dar opens the Gulf and Asia — including large halal markets.

Lobito — the western gate

The Benguela railway from the Angolan Atlantic port of Lobito through the DRC toward Zambia. In its first full year (2025) it moved ~200,000 tonnes, targeting ~1 million tonnes/year by 2030, with a planned greenfield spur into Zambia.12 Lobito shortens the route to Europe and the Americas — the premium markets.

The real conversion job Making mineral rails "beef rails" is not new trunk line. It is reefer capability — refrigerated containers on flat wagons, with power at the yards — plus livestock-loading points and rail-served cold hubs. The two greenfield needs are a Namwala/feedlot spur and rail into Western Province once its disease status allows.
05

The cold chain that has to be built

Beef is a perishable. From the killing floor to the ship, the temperature can never break — a lapse doesn't delay the product, it condemns it. That single constraint defines the logistics network.

A $10B chain is three tiers: live animals moved from grazing districts to zonal export abattoirs; blast-freezing and chilled storage at rail-served hubs; and reefer containers carried by rail and sea to port cold stores at Dar es Salaam and Lobito — with air freight from Lusaka (KKIA) for the highest-value chilled cuts.

06

The binding constraint: power

Here is where the plan meets Zambia's hardest reality. A cold chain needs continuous, reliable electricity — and Zambia's grid, 82% hydro, has been in crisis.

The 2024 drought dropped Lake Kariba's live storage to ~7.7% of usable capacity; ZESCO load-shedding reached up to 21 hours a day, with generation near 900 MW against ~2,400 MW of demand.13,14 For minerals, an outage is lost output. For beef, an outage can be lost product.

So the beef chain cannot be a grid dependent. Each hub needs firm, mostly on-site power: solar PV + battery storage + grid + captive backup. Zambia's ~5–6 kWh/m²/day of sunshine makes this natural — and initiatives like the Chisamba solar plant and a 1,000 MW solar PPA point the way.14

07

What it costs, and who pays

Reaching $10B is a capital program on the order of US$15–25 billion over 25–30 years — and most of it is biological, not concrete.

The single largest line is the herd itself: restocking, genetics and artificial insemination, pasture, water, dip tanks and the extension services that cut mortality and lift off-take. That capital compounds slowly, over cattle generations. The market-access gate — a national livestock ID & traceability system and foot-and-mouth zoning, on the Botswana/Namibia model that unlocked the EU15,16 — is comparatively cheap but absolutely necessary: no traceability, no premium markets.

08

A three-phase path

The route to $10B runs through the $1B target, not around it — the same heartland, rails and disease zones scale up in sequence.

PHASE 1
→ $1B
2026 – 2031 · Prove it
  • Consolidate the Southern Province heartland on the existing spine
  • Stand up a disease-free compartment/zone + pilot national traceability
  • 2–3 EU/halal export abattoirs: Kalomo, Choma, Monze
  • Seed reefer fleet; TAZARA reefer service to Dar for Gulf/halal
  • Grow regional (DRC) volume via road + early Lobito
PHASE 2
→ $4–5B
2031 – 2040 · Scale the herd
  • Herd to ~15–20M via genetics, finishing and mortality control
  • Extend disease zoning; bring Western Province in behind vaccination
  • Build Namwala / feedlot rail spurs; Lobito Zambia spur online
  • Port cold stores at Dar and Lobito at commercial scale
PHASE 3
→ $10B
2040 – 2055 · Top-ten exporter
  • Herd ~40–46M; national traceability; off-take near 28%
  • Both ocean gates at full reefer capacity
  • Premium "Zambian grass-fed" brand into EU, Gulf, Asia, Americas
  • Beef becomes a second export pillar beside copper
