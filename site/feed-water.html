Feeding the herd

You cannot grow a herd from 5 to 40 million on rain and luck. Feed and water are the silent constraint on the whole plan — and the same drought that empties Kariba empties the cattle troughs.

The edge  A maize & soya surplus most beef nations lack The risk  A five-month dry-season feed gap Cost  ≈ $9B — the biggest line in the build
01

The dry-season gap

Zambia's cattle eat well for half the year and starve slowly for the other half. Communal grazing tracks the rains — lush from November, gone by August.

That seasonal collapse in forage is why calves die, cows skip a calving, and carcasses stay light. Closing it — with planted pasture, fodder banks, silage, crop residues and finishing rations — is what converts a subsistence herd into an export herd. It is the single biggest lever on off-take and carcass weight.

02

Zambia's quiet advantage: grain

Here is what Botswana and Namibia don't have and can't easily build: a domestic grain surplus to finish cattle on.

Zambia grew 3.7 million tonnes of maize in 2024/25 and a record 5.1 million tonnes the following season — roughly 4.2 Mt available after seed and other uses — with a national target of 10 Mt by 2031. Soya, the protein in every feed ration, was 0.48 Mt and is targeted at 3 Mt.5,6 A finishing industry runs on exactly this: maize energy, soya-cake protein. The feed base is a policy choice Zambia is already making.

03

Water is the harder constraint

A beef animal drinks 55–112 litres a day. Multiply by a herd heading for 40 million and water, not grass, becomes the limit.

At a working average of ~60 litres per head per day, today's herd already needs about 300 million litres daily; a $10B herd would need roughly 2.4 billion litres a day. In a country where the 2024 drought cut hydropower to a trickle, that water can't be assumed — it has to be built: boreholes, dams, and solar-powered pumping that runs whether or not the grid does. The cold chain and the cattle troughs need the same thing — firm, off-grid solar.

04

Rangeland: graze it without wrecking it

Natural range in much of Zambia carries one animal per many hectares. Planted pasture carries several times more — the shift from extensive to managed grazing is how the land supports a bigger herd.

Manage the commons

Rotational grazing that leaves 40–50% of forage to regrow, water points that spread grazing pressure, and fodder trees on the Kafue Flats and Barotse floodplains where cattle already move with the seasons. Overgrazing is the fast way to lose the whole asset.

Plant the difference

Improved pasture, legumes and dedicated fodder banks multiply carrying capacity on a fraction of the land — and bridge the dry-season gap. This is the productivity lever that lets the herd grow without simply clearing more bush.
