Correction first: it's the Ruperts

■ Attribution flag — high confidence

There is no Oppenheimer cigarette business. The Oppenheimer family fortune is diamonds and gold — Anglo American and De Beers. The Southern-African family that built a global cigarette empire is the Rupert family (Rembrandt → Rothmans → BAT / Richemont). The two are routinely confused; this memo treats the Rupert lineage as the substantive answer.

OPPENHEIMER — diamonds & gold

Ernest Oppenheimer founded Anglo American (1917) and the family controlled De Beers for three generations, exiting its De Beers stake to Anglo American in 2012 for ~USD 5.1B. Mining, minerals, finance — not tobacco.

RUPERT — tobacco & luxury

Anton Rupert founded the Rembrandt Group (1948) on tobacco in Stellenbosch. It grew into Rothmans International, merged into British American Tobacco in 1999, while the family's luxury arm became Richemont (Cartier, Montblanc). Holding vehicles today: Remgro, Reinet, Richemont.

◆ The thread that connects both questions

The irony that makes this one memo, not two: Rothmans — a brand born of the Rupert tobacco empire — is one of the flagship brands rolling off BAT Zambia's Lusaka line today. The family history and the Zambian factory floor meet on the same pack.

BAT Zambia + Roland Imperial Tobacco

British American Tobacco Zambia (LuSE: BATZ) is the dominant manufacturer, operating a state-of-the-art USD 25M+ hub in the Lusaka South Multi-Facility Economic Zone (MFEZ), producing Dunhill, Rothmans and Pall Mall for domestic and export markets.

Roland Imperial Tobacco also opened a facility in the zone. On opening (2018), the MFEZ plants were reported capable of 20M+ cigarettes a day.

The magnet was fiscal: the economic-zone incentive offered investors 0% corporate tax for the first five years of operation — a headline that has since become the memo's central controversy (§04).

Manufactured output is aimed at both the domestic market and regional export; cigarette and leaf exports earn the foreign currency that makes the sector attractive to the finance ministry.

Zambia's own Ministry of Health noted in 2019 that tobacco represents only a small percentage of total agricultural exports and employs only ~0.5% of small and medium-scale farmers — a figure that undercuts the "tobacco = rural jobs" argument often used to defend the incentives. Much of the leaf, and some manufactured product, is exported; those hard-currency earnings are the pull.4,5,6

04

The controversy: policy incoherence

This is the most cited part of the Zambia story — and the most analytically interesting.

Zambia is a party to the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), which commits it to reducing tobacco use. Yet its investment and tax policy — the MFEZ 0% tax holiday — simultaneously subsidised tobacco manufacturing. Researchers describe this as textbook "policy incoherence": the health ministry works to curb tobacco while the finance/investment side gives away revenue to expand its production, chasing export foreign exchange.

▲ The case for (finance / investment)

FDI, a modern manufacturing hub, export earnings in hard currency, and the incentive was offered to all zone investors — not tobacco-specific.

▼ The case against (health / fiscal)

Foregone tax revenue, expanded supply of a harmful product, contradiction with FCTC commitments, and thin employment upside (~0.5% of SME farmers).

◆ Mutambo read

The Zambia case is used internationally as a cautionary tale on tax incentives for cigarette makers. The tension is structural: export FX and factory optics on one side, public-health treaty obligations and foregone revenue on the other. It is a governance/coherence problem more than a market one.4,5,7
